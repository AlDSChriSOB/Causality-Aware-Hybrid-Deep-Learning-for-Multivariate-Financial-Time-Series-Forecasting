# Paper 2 — Final Conclusion

This study investigated whether a leakage-aware Granger-informed graph could improve next-day S&P 500 return forecasting when integrated into a neural message-passing architecture. The experimental design used chronological training, validation and untouched test partitions, training-only graph estimation, rolling-origin graph re-estimation, validation-only hyperparameter selection, matched no-graph and random-graph ablations, and paired HAC forecast-comparison tests with Holm correction.

The final graph message-passing model used 122 directed training-only edges among 14 input variables. On 3,807 untouched test observations, it achieved an RMSE of 0.01120, compared with 0.01132 for the matched no-graph model and 0.01120 for the random-graph control. Thus, graph message passing improved the model relative to removing graph messages, but the improvement was not attributable to the Granger-derived topology: the graph model and random-graph control had virtually identical RMSE.

The paired HAC tests provide the decisive inference. Graph message passing versus no graph produced p = 0.5316 and Holm-adjusted p = 1.0000. Graph message passing versus the random-graph control produced p = 0.9732 and Holm-adjusted p = 1.0000. Comparisons against the training-mean and zero-return baselines produced Holm-adjusted p-values of 0.4538 in both cases. None of the predeclared comparisons therefore rejects equal expected squared-error loss at the conventional 5% level.

The principal conclusion is consequently negative/null: **under the present data, target, feature set, graph-estimation procedure and neural architecture, the Granger-informed graph does not provide statistically supported improvement in next-day S&P 500 return forecasting.** The study demonstrates a reproducible way to integrate a predictive-dependence graph into a forecasting architecture without temporal leakage, but it does not establish that the estimated graph topology contains forecasting information beyond what can be obtained from an equally sized random topology or from the underlying neural representation.

This finding should not be generalized to all causality-aware or graph-based financial forecasting. Granger predictive dependence is not equivalent to structural causal effect, and the present result is conditional on the modelling and evaluation choices. Future research should investigate dynamic and window-specific graph estimation, alternative causal-discovery methods, richer temporal sequence models, economically motivated variables, longer forecast horizons, and independent financial datasets. Any future claim of graph-specific forecasting benefit should require improvement against topology-matched controls and statistical evidence on genuinely untouched out-of-sample periods.

The appropriate contribution of Paper 2 is therefore methodological and empirical: it provides a leakage-aware evaluation framework and shows, through matched controls and corrected statistical testing, that an apparently plausible Granger-informed graph advantage does not survive rigorous out-of-sample validation in this S&P 500 forecasting setting.
